Planning the job before anyone lifts a wrench

Most installation problems trace back to decisions made too late. A short checklist of questions settles most of them.

  1. 1

    Describe the space

    Tell us where the heater will sit: basement, closet, garage or utility room. Note the ceiling height, door width and any stairs the unit has to travel.

  2. 2

    Decide on the fuel

    Natural gas, electric or heat pump each need different connections. The cheapest to buy is not always the cheapest to run.

  3. 3

    Size it to the household

    Count the people, the bathrooms and the biggest simultaneous demand, such as a shower and a dishwasher running together.

  4. 4

    Check the venting path

    Gas units need a safe way to exhaust combustion gases, and the old flue may not suit a new model.

  5. 5

    Book the permit and a date

    The city lists hot water heaters under plumbing permits, so the permit and inspection fit into the schedule.

Sizing: why bigger is not automatically better

A tank's first-hour rating tells you how much hot water it can deliver in a busy hour, which matters more than its gallon label. A family with long showers and a soaking tub wants a higher rating than two adults who shower briefly. An oversized tank wastes energy keeping extra water hot all day.

Recovery speed matters as much as size. Gas heaters reheat quickly, standard electric tanks recover more slowly, and heat pump models are slower still in efficiency mode. If your household has peak-time demand, choose by first-hour delivery, not by tank volume alone.

Venting, combustion air and the gas line

A gas heater must carry exhaust gas out of the house safely. Natural draft models rely on a chimney or a vertical vent that rises steadily, while power-vent models push exhaust through plastic pipe, even through a side wall. A water heater that backdrafts can put carbon monoxide into the house, so venting is not a place to cut corners.

Combustion air matters as well. A heater in a tight closet needs vents or ducting to draw in enough air, otherwise the flame burns poorly. When a furnace and heater share a flue, a new heater can change how both draft, so we check the whole path.

The gas line gets checked for size and a sediment trap, plus a shutoff valve within reach. If you want a larger unit or a tankless model, the existing pipe may need upgrading.

Expansion tanks, pressure and the other small parts

When cold water heats up it expands, and in a closed system it has nowhere to go. An expansion tank gives that water room so the pressure does not spike. Without one, a relief valve may drip, fittings may wear and appliances may fail early.

A new install is also the time for fresh shutoff valves, flexible connectors, a drain pan with a drain line and a relief discharge line that ends where it can be seen. These parts cost little and prevent the most common installation callbacks.

The permit path through the city

The City of Wyoming lists hot water heaters among the work needing a plumbing permit before it starts. You apply online through BS&A or with a paper form, and the invoice is paid before the permit is issued. Contractors register first, and homeowners need not.

To pull the permit you must hold the Michigan license for the work or be the owner and resident of the dwelling. The permit holder schedules inspections with at least 24 hours' notice, so build a day for that into your plan. Skipping the permit can complicate a home sale or an insurance claim later.

Existing home or new build: what changes

Replacing in an existing house

  • Work around the current gas, vent and water lines
  • Closet or basement size limits the model
  • Old shutoffs and unions often need replacing
  • Often the same fuel as before

New construction or remodel

  • Run the vent and circuit where you want them
  • Choose a hybrid or tankless without retrofit limits
  • Plan the drain, pan and expansion tank early
  • Coordinate rough-in and final inspections

Planning to switch from gas to a heat pump tank

A heat pump heater draws warmth from the room air, so it likes a space of decent volume and a nearby drain for condensate. It also needs a circuit sized for the unit, and your electrical panel must have room. We confirm these before ordering anything.

If the panel or the room is not ready, a standard tank now and a hybrid later can be the better sequence. Electrical panel work is outside plumbing, so a licensed electrician handles that part.

Reasons homeowners schedule an install

Old tank still working

A heater past its middle years can fail without warning. Planning the swap avoids a flooded basement and a rushed purchase.

Remodel or addition

New bathrooms raise demand. A larger tank, a second unit or a tankless model may suit the new load.

Fuel switch

Some owners move from gas to electric or heat pump for efficiency or to retire a gas appliance.

Service flush and tune-up

If a flush shows the tank is badly scaled, we help you decide whether to keep it or replace it on your timeline.
