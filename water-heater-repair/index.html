What the symptom is telling you

The way a heater misbehaves narrows the cause quickly. These are the patterns we see most often.

No hot water at all

On an electric tank this points to a tripped limit switch, a bad thermostat or a burned element. On gas, the pilot, thermocouple or igniter is the usual suspect.

Hot water, but only briefly

A broken dip tube lets incoming cold water mix near the top of the tank. A failed lower element on an electric unit gives the same result.

Water that is too hot or swings

A stuck thermostat or a sensor out of calibration can overheat the tank. The relief valve may then discharge, which is a safety response, not the cause.

Rotten egg or metallic smell

Bacteria in the tank react with the anode rod and produce the odor. Replacing or swapping the rod and flushing the tank often clears it.

Knocking, popping or hissing

Sediment on the tank floor boils the trapped water. A flush may quiet it, though heavy scale can mean the tank is near the end of its life.

Water on the floor

Drips from the relief valve, a drain valve or a pipe joint are repairable. Water from the bottom seam of the tank is not, and that tank needs replacing.

Gas, electric and hybrid heaters: what actually breaks

On a gas heater the burner assembly does the work, and a thermocouple or flame sensor tells the control that a flame is present. A dirty burner, a weak thermocouple or a draft problem makes the flame drop out, so the unit shuts down. The fix is typically cleaning or replacing a small part, and it takes far less time than a full swap.

Electric heaters use two elements, one near the top and one near the bottom, each controlled by a thermostat. When the upper one burns out you get no hot water, and when the lower one fails you get a short supply. Testing with a meter shows which part has died.

Hybrid heat pump models add a compressor, a fan, an air filter and a condensate drain, so symptoms can be as plain as a clogged filter or a blocked drain line. They also have a backup element, which is why a hybrid can seem fine in standard mode and quit in efficiency mode.

Parts that wear out, and the ones we check first

The anode rod is a sacrificial metal rod that corrodes so the steel tank does not. Once it is spent, the tank starts to rust. Checking it every few years extends the life of a good heater, especially where water is hard.

The temperature and pressure relief valve, often called the T&P valve, protects against overheating and excess pressure. It should open and close cleanly. A valve that weeps constantly may point to high water pressure or a missing expansion tank.

The thermostat and upper limit switch on an electric tank are safety devices as well as controls. A tripped reset button usually means something else has failed, so we test the circuit before resetting it.

Energy suppliers and the permit question

Wyoming homes get natural gas from DTE Energy and electricity from Consumers Energy. If your heater has no flame or no power, check that a gas appliance shutoff or a breaker has not tripped before calling, and tell the dispatcher what you found. If you smell gas, leave the house and contact the gas utility first.

The City of Wyoming lists hot water heaters among the plumbing work that needs a permit. A straight repair, such as a new element or thermocouple, is a different job from a replacement, so ask us if your visit turns into a swap. If it does, we explain the permit and the 24-hour inspection notice before anything is installed.

Safety steps for a failing water heater

  • ✓If you smell gas, leave and call 911 or DTE Energy from outside.
  • ✓Turn the power off at the breaker, or the gas control to off.
  • ✓Shut the cold supply valve to the heater.
  • ✓Never cap or plug the temperature and pressure relief valve.
  • ✓Keep hot discharge away from skin because water can scald.
  • ✓Keep flammables clear of the area.
