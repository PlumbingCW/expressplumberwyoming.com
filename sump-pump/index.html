A sump pump collects groundwater in a pit below the basement floor and pushes it outside through a discharge line before it can rise to floor level. Sump pump repair in Wyoming usually means a switch, a motor, a check valve or a frozen line.

Why spring thaw and freeze-thaw cycles load the pump

Michigan winters leave frozen ground that sheds water instead of absorbing it. When spring snowmelt arrives along with rain, the ground around a foundation fills quickly, and a pump that sat idle for months must suddenly run for hours. Freeze-thaw cycles can also crack or heave drain tile and shift the discharge line.

This is why many failures cluster in late winter and early spring. A pump that has not been tested since fall is the one most likely to stick on its first long run of the season.

How sump pumps fail

Float switch

The float rises with the water and tells the pump to start. If it jams on the pit wall or tangles with its own cord, the pump either never runs or never stops.

Motor burnout

Pumps that cycle constantly or run dry overheat. Silt and debris in the pit speed this up.

Failed check valve

The check valve stops water from draining back into the pit after each cycle. If it fails, you hear a thump and the pump short-cycles.

Frozen or blocked discharge

If the outdoor end of the line freezes or clogs, the pump runs but the water has nowhere to go. A discharge that ends too close to the house also returns water to the foundation.

Power loss

Storms that take out Consumers Energy service at the wrong time stop an electric pump. Wyoming homes without a backup lose protection exactly when the ground is wettest, so a battery system is worth considering.

Battery backup versus relying on one pump

A single AC pump

  • Lowest upfront cost.
  • Stops when the power does.
  • Works well if outages are rare and the water table is low.
  • Needs periodic testing.

AC pump plus battery backup

  • Keeps pumping through an outage.
  • Needs the battery replaced on a schedule.
  • Gives a second pump if the primary fails.
  • Costs more upfront.

Testing your pump before the thaw

A short check in fall or late winter catches most problems early.

  1. 1

    Inspect the pit

    Lift the lid. Look for silt, gravel or debris and clear what you can reach.

  2. 2

    Pour in water

    Slowly add a bucket of water until the float rises. The pump should start and empty the pit.

  3. 3

    Watch the cycle

    Check that the pump shuts off cleanly and the check valve does not slam.

  4. 4

    Check the outside end

    Make sure the discharge is clear of snow, ice and leaves, and that it empties away from the foundation.

  5. 5

    Test the backup

    If you have a battery system, press its test button and confirm the alarm works.

Sewage ejector pumps are a different job

A sump pump handles groundwater. An ejector pump handles sewage from a basement bathroom or laundry that sits below the sewer line. The two are not interchangeable, and the pit must be sealed and vented for an ejector. If the pump serves a toilet, a failure means sewage backing up in the basement.

Call right away if an ejector alarm sounds. For the broader question of keeping groundwater out, see our basement waterproofing page.

New installs and extending the discharge

A new installation means a pit, a pump, a check valve and a discharge route out of the house. If the discharge needs to run farther from the foundation, someone has to dig, so call MISS DIG 811 before any digging starts. We also check for leaks around the pit that might be mistaken for groundwater with our leak detection methods.
