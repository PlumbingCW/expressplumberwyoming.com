Hydro jetting in Wyoming means cleaning a drain or sewer line with high-pressure water instead of a spinning cable. A nozzle on a hose sends jets backward to scour the pipe wall and forward to pull itself along. It is the tool we reach for when ordinary snaking has not held.

Signs a snake is not enough

Cable machines are fast and fine for many clogs. These patterns tell us the problem is bigger than a single plug.

The clog returns

Cleared in spring, slow again by fall. A cable cuts a hole through the blockage but leaves most of the coating on the pipe, so the line narrows again.

The snake stalls

The cable goes in, hits something, and will not move on. That could be a mass of roots, a heavy scale ring or a pipe problem, and each needs a different answer.

Slow drains all over the house

When the tub, kitchen sink and washer all drag together, the buildup is often spread through the main rather than sitting in one spot.

Roots keep growing back

A cutting head clears a channel through roots, but the root mass remains. Water pressure removes far more of it from the pipe wall.

Grease from a kitchen or restaurant line

Cooled fat bonds to pipe walls in layers. Water at high pressure peels it away instead of boring through the center.

What roots, grease and scale actually do inside a pipe

Roots find the small gaps at pipe joints, drawn by moisture and nutrients. Once inside, they grow into a mat that catches paper and waste. Jetting with a root-cutting nozzle can remove the mat across a longer stretch than a cable can.

Grease behaves differently. It forms a smooth, thick lining that tightens the opening from every side, so a snake punches a small hole and water finds a way through for a while. Jetting strips the lining so the pipe returns closer to its original diameter.

Scale is mineral buildup, and it is rougher and harder. Cast iron develops a crusty interior over decades, and that surface catches debris like a net. Jetting can knock down a good portion of it, though heavily scaled pipe may also be thin, which is the reason for a camera look first.

Older cast iron and clay lines need a look first

Many Wyoming houses date to the 1950s building boom, and the sewer lines under them may be cast iron or clay. These materials can be brittle, cracked or partly collapsed after decades in the ground, and strong water pressure aimed at a failing pipe can make a bad spot worse.

That is why we may run a camera before we jet an older line. Our sewer camera inspection shows the pipe condition, and the result decides whether jetting is safe or whether repair has to come first.

When jetting is the wrong tool

Jetting helps when

  • The pipe is intact but coated with grease or scale.
  • Roots entered at a joint that is otherwise sound.
  • A cable has cleared it before and the clog returned.
  • You want the line clean before a camera inspection.

Skip jetting when

  • A camera shows a collapsed or crushed section.
  • The pipe is cracked, offset or separated at a joint.
  • The line has a belly holding standing water.
  • A blockage is a foreign object that needs retrieval, not scouring.

Jetting findings and the drain rules that follow

Jetting clears a line without altering it, so a cleaning visit generally needs no permit. If the camera then shows a broken pipe, the repair falls under the 2021 Michigan Plumbing Code, based on the 2021 IPC, and Wyoming requires a permit for drain replacement.

Ask to see the footage before agreeing to any repair, and confirm the plumber's state license on the LARA lookup.
