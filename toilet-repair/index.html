A toilet is a simple machine: a tank that stores water, a valve that refills it, a flapper that releases it, and a trap that carries waste away. Almost every failure is one of those four parts.

What fails inside the fixture

Match the symptom to the part and you can usually tell whether the fix is small.

Flapper

The rubber seal at the bottom of the tank lets water slip into the bowl when it warps or gets coated with film. The tank refills over and over, and you hear the toilet run on its own.

Fill valve

This is the tall assembly that refills the tank and shuts off at a set level. When it sticks or its seal wears, water never stops entering and spills down the overflow tube.

Wax ring and flange

The wax ring seals the toilet to the drain flange under the bowl. A leak at the floor, a slight rock, or a faint sewer smell usually points here.

Trap and drain

A blocked trap gives a slow flush, gurgling or a bowl that fills and sits. Plunging helps with paper blockages, while a toy, wipe or buildup needs an auger.

Supply line and shutoff

The braided line and the small valve behind the toilet can drip at the connection or stick closed. A valve that will not turn is a repair in its own right.

Toilet overflowing or leaking onto the floor

Overflowing water carries waste, so treat the room as contaminated. Do not flush again, and keep children and pets out until it is cleaned and disinfected.

If the supply valve will not turn, or water comes from the floor rather than the bowl, call right away. After-hours and weekend calls get the same triage as daytime ones, and we offer same-day appointments when available for problems that can hold until morning.

When a toilet job turns into permit work

Replacing a flapper or fill valve is plain repair. Resetting a toilet on a new flange, moving it, or adding a bathroom involves drains and fixtures, which Wyoming covers with a permit and the 2021 Michigan Plumbing Code, based on the 2021 IPC.

You must be licensed by the State of Michigan for that work or be the owner and resident of the home.

What you can do and where to stop

Some toilet fixes are safe for a homeowner. These are the lines we suggest not crossing.

Wax rings, flanges and floor leaks

The wax ring is a soft gasket between the toilet and the floor drain. When the toilet rocks, the ring compresses unevenly and can let water seep out with each flush, often soaking the subfloor over time. Replacing it means lifting the toilet, cleaning the flange and setting everything level again.

The flange is the metal or plastic ring screwed to the floor. If it is cracked, corroded or sitting too low, a new ring will not seal it, and the repair becomes a flange fix. Soft flooring around the base is a sign to call sooner than later.

Mineral buildup and the Lake Michigan supply

City and neighboring utility reports put hardness in the 140s ppm, roughly 8 to 9 grains per gallon, so test your tap. In practice, that means mineral scale gradually coats the flapper seat, the fill valve and the fill hole under the rim. A toilet that starts to run after a few years may need a cleaned seat and a new flapper rather than a new fixture.

Fixing the old toilet versus fitting a new one

Repair makes sense when

  • The bowl and tank are intact.
  • One part has failed.
  • The toilet flushes strongly when it works.
  • You like the height and shape.

Replacement makes sense when

  • Hairline cracks show in the china.
  • Parts keep failing in sequence.
  • The flush needs repeated attempts.
  • You want a taller or more efficient model.

New installs, moves and permits

Swapping a toilet in the same spot is straightforward. Adding a new toilet or moving one is adding a fixture, and Wyoming Building Inspections requires a plumbing permit before that work starts. Tell us at the start, because the permit and the inspection date shape the schedule. Our installation service covers new bathrooms and fixture additions.
