Tank leaking onto the floor right now

A slow weep from a fitting is one problem. Water coming from the bottom of the jacket or pooling under the whole tank usually means the steel shell has rusted through, and no repair closes that. The tank will not heal, and the leak tends to grow when the heater cycles.

Stopping the water matters more than saving the heater. Every hour a failed tank keeps filling, it keeps feeding the leak, and drywall, flooring and stored boxes soak it up. We are a plumbing crew, so drying and restoration work is outside what we do, which is one more reason to cut the supply fast.

  • 1Close the cold water inlet valve on top of the tank, or the main shutoff if you cannot find it.
  • 2Turn a gas heater's control knob to off, or switch the electric heater's breaker off at the panel.
  • 3Do not touch the heater or any wet cord while standing in water.
  • 4Open one hot tap upstairs to let pressure ease and slow the drip.
  • 5Move boxes, rugs and electronics out of the spill path, then call (855) 575-2890.

Signs the tank is finished

Most tanks give warnings before they fail. If you see two or more of these, plan the swap before the floor gets wet.

Damp or rusty base

Rust streaks around the bottom ring or flaking on the jacket point to a shell that is corroding from the inside out.

Popping and rumbling

Mineral scale settles on the tank floor and traps water beneath it. The water flashes to steam and makes the noise, and the scale cooks the lining.

Brown or metallic hot water

Cold taps run clear while hot taps look rusty. The anode rod has likely been consumed and the steel is now being eaten instead.

Hot water runs out fast

Sediment takes up space in the tank and a failing element or burner cannot keep up, so showers go cold sooner than they used to.

Moisture on the floor with no plumbing nearby

Check the drain pan and the relief valve discharge pipe before assuming the tank is gone. A wet pan points to the tank, a fitting or the relief valve, so dry it and watch where the water returns from.

Gas, electric or power vent: matching the new tank to the house

Wyoming homes rely on natural gas from DTE Energy and electricity from Consumers Energy, so the choice is rarely about availability. It is about what the old heater was connected to and what is practical to change.

Tankless units need larger gas lines and different venting, so tankless service is a planned upgrade rather than an emergency swap. Staying with the same fuel is almost always the fastest path on an urgent swap, because the gas line, vent or circuit is already there.

If you would rather plan a fuel change, our planned water heater installation page covers sizing and venting.

A standard atmospheric gas heater vents by natural draft, which needs a chimney or a rated vent run up through the roof. If the old flue is oversized or lined poorly, it can leave condensation and backdraft problems with a new, more efficient tank. A power-vent model uses a fan to push exhaust out through plastic pipe, which opens up the option of venting through a side wall.

Electric tanks need no vent at all, only a dedicated circuit of the right size and a space with room around the unit. They cost less to buy and install, but they typically cost more to run where gas is cheap. We tell you the trade-off plainly, and the final call is yours.

Where a heat pump (hybrid) tank fits

A hybrid heater pulls heat from the surrounding air and moves it into the tank, using far less electricity than resistance elements. It works best in a basement or utility room with decent volume of air and a condensate drain nearby, and it cools and dries the room slightly as it runs.

It also stands taller than a standard tank, makes some fan noise, and needs the right electrical supply. On an emergency swap we can still set one if the space and circuit allow it, but sometimes the right answer is a standard tank now and a hybrid when you can plan the upgrade.

Why hard Lake Michigan water shortens a tank's life

Wyoming has drawn its drinking water from Lake Michigan since 1966, and city and neighboring utility reports put hardness in the 140s ppm, roughly 8 to 9 grains per gallon. That is moderately hard to hard water, and every heating cycle drops a little scale onto the tank floor and the elements. Test your tap, and if the number is high, ask about a softener or a regular flush so the next tank lasts longer.

A flush once a year is cheap insurance on a new unit. If the tank is not the problem and only an element or thermostat has failed, a water heater repair may be the cheaper answer. It clears the sediment before it hardens into a crust, keeps the burner or lower element working directly on water instead of through scale, and lets you spot a failing anode rod early.

Older houses, older tanks: what the 1950s boom left behind

Much of Wyoming grew fast in the 1950s, after the 28th Street corridor was widened following the approval of US-131's route in 1952. Many homes from that era have had their water heater replaced at least once, often with a quick swap that reused the original venting and old shutoff valves. Corroded unions, undersized flue pipe and a missing drain pan are common things to check when a heater area is opened up.

A replacement is the right moment to fix those. New shutoff valves, flexible connectors, a proper relief discharge line and a drain pan with a drain path cost little against the tank, and they stop the next failure from becoming a flood.

The city permit and what it means for your swap

The City of Wyoming's Building Inspections page lists hot water heaters among the work that needs a plumbing permit before it starts. Permits are applied for online through BS&A or on a printed form, and the invoice is paid before the permit is issued. The permit holder schedules inspections with at least 24 hours' notice.

In practice this means a replacement is not finished when the water runs hot. A licensed plumber pulls the permit and books the inspection, and a homeowner doing the work personally must own and live in the dwelling. If you have been told a swap needs no permit, ask which office said so, since the city's own page says otherwise.
